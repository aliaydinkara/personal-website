Banditry and Injustice in Early Modern Empires: The Köroğlu Epic from Iran to Anatolia

Book project — Edinburgh University Press, forthcoming 2027

This historical monograph explores how the popular epic of the bandit-hero Köroğlu, a trans-regional tradition previously studied primarily by scholars of folklore and literature, contains subversive and anti-state historical perspectives on the Ottoman and Safavid Empires. Connecting the tradition to the social and economic history of banditry, rebellion, and tribal autonomy, the study situates it within comparative debates on outlawry and resistance in the early modern world.

Drawing on the earliest written renditions of the epic, recorded in nineteenth-century Iran and Anatolia but reflecting an oral tradition that emerged in the seventeenth century, the book examines the limits of imperial expansion and legitimacy in the Ottoman-Safavid borderlands. The protagonist's Turkoman identity preserves important cultural memories of the tribal coalition that built the Safavid state and was subsequently marginalized by its consolidation. Read against official court chronicles, the tradition offers an alternative history of imperial power, popular dissent, and the social worlds of the early modern Middle East.

Mapping Rebellion in Seventeenth-Century Ottoman Lands

Ongoing research — Historical economic geography

This project investigates the causes and long-term consequences of uprising, rebellion, and instability in the seventeenth-century Ottoman Empire, with particular attention to the Celali events. I am building an original dataset of uprisings and related events from Ottoman archival sources, with the aim of georeferencing and mapping their geographical distribution and chronology. Combining historical research with spatial and quantitative analysis, the project seeks to identify the economic, social, and political conditions associated with uprising and explore its lasting effects on the regions affected.
